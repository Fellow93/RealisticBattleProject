# RBM Campaign Module

It gives every stack of troops in your party its own purse, fills it with the gear they strip off the battlefield and a share of their wages, and makes them spend it on their own upgrades, their own food, and their own drink.

And it makes that gear matter where you never see it: battles resolved on the map are now decided by what your men are actually wearing and carrying, not by the tier number beside their name.

## Upgrades are priced by the kit

Vanilla charges a flat, hand-authored gold number to upgrade a troop. RBM replaces that with the actual value of the equipment the man gains — his new armor, weapons, shield and horse, minus what he already carried, averaged over all the loadouts that troop type can spawn with. A recruit becoming a footman is cheap. A veteran stepping into heavy mail and a warhorse is not. (All the vanilla perks that discount upgrades — Sound Reserves, Renowned Archer, Contractors, the Khuzait cavalry feat — still apply on top.)

And troops can only upgrade where there's a town to outfit them. A stack upgrades only while a friendly or neutral town sits within reach on the map — or while it rests in any friendly settlement, which sends to the nearest city for the kit — and the gear its gold buys comes off that town's shelves. March too far from friendly ground and your men make do with what they have until you bring them back within a short ride of a market. (The AI's men whose own spoils cover their promotion re-arm out of their loot and are not held back by distance; only the gold-bought ones wait for a town. Turn `TroopUpgradeRequireSupplyTown` off to upgrade anywhere, the way vanilla does.)

A troop stepping up to a mounted tier is priced its horse and harness in gold like any other kit, rather than needing a live horse pulled from your baggage train — none is consumed. (Turn `TroopUpgradeChargeMountValue` off to restore vanilla's horse-item requirement.)

## Wages scale with tier

Vanilla pays a soldier 1 to 23 gold a day off a hand-authored table that barely moves across the whole tree. RBM replaces it with the medieval daily rates — pence at ten gold to the penny — and a soldier's keep roughly doubles every couple of rungs instead:

| tier | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| foot | 20 | 30 | 40 | 60 | 120 | 240 |
| horse | 30 | 40 | 60 | 120 | 240 | 480 |

A horseman draws what a footman one tier above him does, which is the other thing vanilla never priced. Tier 0 rabble are paid as tier 1 — nobody serves for nothing.

There is no dial on this. The table is the wage, and it applies whenever the campaign module is on; turn the module off for vanilla pay.

## Spoils: where the money comes from

Each troop stack (say, your 40 Imperial Recruits) holds a stockpile of **spoils**, measured in gold. One point of spoils is one gold piece — it just lives in the soldiers' pockets rather than yours.

Several things fill it:

**Winning battles.** Hold the field and your men strip the dead — the enemy's dead *and* your own fallen, horses and harness included. The wounded keep their kit (they're carried off wearing it) and routers fled with theirs. Nothing comes off a field intact: every piece salvages a random quarter to three-quarters of its worth, because armor is battered, blades are chipped, and a quiver is only worth the arrows still in it. The enemy's own purse is part of the prize too — most of what their fallen and wounded were carrying is captured with the field, the rest lost in the rout. Your share of it all scales with how much your party actually contributed to the battle.

Your own dead take their share of their stack's purse to the grave. When a whole stack falls, its comrades recover half its purse off the bodies and split it among themselves, veterans taking the larger cut.

How that loot gets divided is the interesting part. **Veterans pick first**, but each man can only carry so much (three pieces by default), and the further beneath a soldier a piece of gear lies, the likelier he is to walk straight past it — roughly a coin-flip per tier of difference. So the good kit goes to the elite stacks, and the recruits' spears and rags get left lying for the greener troops who'd actually consider them an upgrade. A veteran alone on a field will eventually stoop for a peasant's club, but he won't fill his arms with them while there's mail about.

**Raiding villages.** When your party sacks a village, its soldiers pocket coin and plate as they go, on top of the goods you cart off. The raid strips the village's own purse in proportion to how thoroughly it was looted — a raid you saw through to the end takes it all, one you broke off only its share — and the men carry off half of what was taken; the other half is burned, spoiled or hidden and gone for good. A raid the militia or a relief force turned back pays nothing. The plunder is split among the parties who raided by their part in it, and within each party by rank, veterans taking the larger cut. The goods are wasted the same way: a raid hauls off only about half of what vanilla would hand you, more under a leader schooled in Roguery or a Nord one.

**Besieging and storming.** A siege bleeds its target before the walls ever fall: every day it holds, a twentieth of the town's market wealth (or a castle's treasury) is drained, half of it pocketed by the besiegers and the rest destroyed. Take the place by storm and what happens next is the choice vanilla already asks you to make. **Show mercy** and the men take a tenth of the town's wealth; **pillage** and they take half of it and four-tenths of the market's stock; **devastate** and they take nine-tenths of both — but a burning city keeps almost nothing, so only a tenth of that reaches the men against half under pillage. Whatever is not carried off is destroyed, never banked, and a town's treasury is left untouched to pass to its new owner. The plunder is shared across every party in the siege by its part in it, then within each party by rank. Only a capture by storm sacks the place: a fief handed to you by barter, gift or a council vote leaves its wealth alone.

**Ransoms and prisoners.** Ransom a prisoner in a town — or ransom a captive lord by courier or barter — and his captors keep the whole worth of his arms, armor and horse as spoils, on top of the gold the buying town pays for the man himself. Prisoners you leave on the field after a battle are stripped before they are turned loose, for half their kit's worth. Prisoners handed over to a quest are stripped like ransomed ones.

**Wages.** The whole of what you pay a stack each day goes straight back into its own purse. This doesn't cost you extra gold — it's a statement about where their pay was already going: mending what the march wore through, replacing what couldn't be mended. A mercenary company under contract banks twice its wage, the second wage being the one its employer reimburses. Garrisons bank the wage their fief pays them; a town's militia draws a quarter of a soldier's wage from the town, a castle's a tenth, and a village levy nothing.

**Recruiting.** A man mustered out of a village or town arrives with twenty days of his own upkeep already in his purse, rather than penniless.

## Where the money goes

**Upgrades.** When you upgrade a stack, its spoils are spent one man at a time. If the stockpile covers three men, those three upgrade free and the fourth pays full price out of your treasury. The party screen tooltip spells this out — a **Spoils cover: X** line, a **You pay: Y** line, what the whole batch would cost you, and how much of a mounted promotion is the horse — and there's a spoils bar on each troop card showing how full the stockpile is. The men the stockpile covers re-arm out of their own loot. The ones you pay for are a purchase, not a conjuring: their new kit comes off the supply town's shelves, and the gold you paid goes to that town's merchants, who pay the market fee on it like any other trade. (Each party in the clan screen also carries an optional daily cap on the gold it may spend on upgrades.)

**Maintenance.** Every day in the field a stack pays to keep its own kit serviceable — a small share of everything it carries, horse and harness included, so a lancer in full harness costs more to maintain than a spearman. How much the men are expected to find themselves depends on whose war it is: an **independent** clan's soldiers fund their own keep from their purses, and so does a **mercenary** company in a kingdom's pay, whose doubled wage is what pays for it; a sworn **vassal's** men pay none of it — their liege bears the whole bill, as he agreed to when he took their oath. Whatever the purses don't cover falls to you, drawn from your treasury. That gold isn't burnt: it goes to the nearest town not at war with you — the one you're standing in, if you are — which takes its market fee out of it like any other trade. Villages and castles never take it, however long you sit in one; an armourer who can re-sole a hauberk is a town trade. What the men pay out of their own purses is simply spent. Maintenance buys the armourer's and the farrier's labour, not stock, so it takes nothing off the town's shelves — an army on campaign is a customer, and the towns it passes get rich on it. Garrisons and militia are billed their maintenance through their settlement instead. (Set `TroopMaintenanceFraction` to zero to stop it.)

**Food.** When your party stops in a town or village, soldiers buy their own rations off the local market — real items, taken from that settlement's actual stock, at that settlement's actual prices. While a stack is carrying its own food, it doesn't eat out of your party's food stores at all. Better-paid troops buy better food: a man will spend up to half a day's wage on a day's rations, so a recruit buys grain and a veteran buys meat and cheese. If a starving village has nothing on the stalls, your men leave hungry.

**Drink.** Every hour your party idles in a settlement, the men spend on taverns, dice and worse — at the default, about a quarter of what the day paid them, so a stay in town slows a stack's saving without emptying it. Push the dial past 1 and they drink more than they earn, and **a garrison parked in a town will drink its way out of ever affording better armor.** They never go into debt; an empty purse spends nothing. A stack already over its cap drinks harder — that surplus is what carousing is really for — though however deep his purse, a man can only drink so much in a day, more as his rank rises. Garrisons and militia carouse too: their purse holds the wage their own settlement paid them, so their drinking hands it back. The coin goes to the settlement, and in a town half of it buys beer, wine and food off the market.

**Luxuries.** A stack already sitting on more spoils than it could ever spend on its own kit will now and then indulge — buying a luxury off the settlement's market (jewellery, velvet, furs, wine, a fine garment) purely to have it. The coin leaves for the settlement the way carousing does, and the good is a personal keepsake rather than party loot: it can't be carted to the next town and sold back for gold. Only over-cap stacks splurge, only once in a while, and never twice in quick succession.

**Healing.** A stack resting in a settlement pays the local surgeons out of its own purse to mend its wounded faster than they'd knit on the march. A veteran costs more to patch up than a recruit, and only a little of the wounded is mended each hour, so a bad convalescence still means a stay in town — it just costs the stack the kit it was saving for. This runs on top of the game's own free daily healing, so an empty-pursed stack still recovers, only slower.

**A commander's cut.** Before the rest settles into the stacks, the party leader skims a share of everything his men gather — off a battlefield, a raid, a siege or a sack, or off ransomed and stripped prisoners — straight into his own purse as gold. A greater lord takes a larger cut: the share is multiplied by his clan's standing, so a landless captain takes it once over and a great dynasty many times it. A mercenary captain under contract takes half again as much, and a leader's Roguery widens it further. This is the one place a purse turns back into treasury gold. (Set `TroopLeaderSpoilsCutFraction` to zero to leave the men everything they take.)

**Companions** strip the field alongside the men, but keep no purse: their share is paid straight into your gold, and no cut is taken from it.

**Stewardship.** A leader whose men provision themselves earns Steward experience for it — for every coin his stacks lay out on their own food and luxuries (not drink), for keeping deep food stores, and for every spare horse in the baggage that could put a footman in the saddle.

Otherwise spoils are a **closed loop**: what a stack loots and earns is spent on its own upgrades, upkeep, food and drink, not handed back as gold. A stack keeps saving toward its next upgrade and a war chest that grows with the men's tier; once it holds more than that, the surplus goes to drink and the odd luxury rather than sitting idle.

## Detailed auto resolve

When a battle resolves on the map rather than on the field — you send your troops in, or two AI parties meet somewhere you aren't — vanilla decides it almost entirely on one number: the troop's **tier**. Its whole strength is `(2 + tier) × (10 + tier) × 0.02`, a blow lands the ratio of the two strengths to the power 0.7, and nothing else about the man enters into it. It does not know what he is wearing. It does not know what he is holding. A tier-1 recruit in mail behind a shield, levelling a spear, hits a tier-0 looter about **1.42×** as hard as the looter swinging a stick in rags hits back — and that thin margin is the entire difference between them.

That is why your recruits could lose a close fight to looters on the map and slaughter the same looters on the field. The two battles were not being decided by the same things.

RBM takes tier out of the reckoning and puts the soldier back in. A tier was only ever shorthand for *what kit does he carry and how well is he trained* — so we measure those directly, from his actual armor, his actual weapon and his actual skill, and drop the shorthand rather than charge for the same thing twice.

**Armor is real armor now.** Every blow is run through the same armor equation the live battle uses — and *which* equation depends on which combat module is running, so that auto-resolve stays faithful to the battle you would actually have fought. With RBM Combat on, armor is roughly twice as protective as vanilla's, and a heavy harness can stop a cut outright, letting nothing through but the blunt shock of it. With RBM Combat off, vanilla's gentler curve is used instead. Either way the numbers come from the game's own item data, not from a table of guesses.

**What you hit with matters.** A mace against a mail hauberk is not a sword against a mail hauberk. Under RBM's armor rules a blunt weapon carries far more of a stopped blow through as trauma, and a spear-point beats armor a sword-edge cannot — so maces and spears come into their own against heavy troops, exactly as they do on the field. A bow is priced with the arrows it looses, not on its own, and it wounds in the arrow's kind.

**And a soldier is not one weapon.** He carries a spear *and* an axe, and he swings whichever happens to be in his hand — so a blow is priced as the average of everything on his belt, not the best of it. This matters more than it sounds: a mace and a sabre answer armor differently, so they are each run through the armor equation separately and averaged *afterwards*. Averaging them into one weapon first would produce a blow that is neither.

Nor is an archer one arrow. An Imperial Archer carries flight arrows in two of his three kits and needle bodkins in the third, and a bodkin punches through a hauberk that a broadhead barely scratches — so the arrow with the bigger number printed on it is very often the *worse* one against a man in mail. Every kind of shaft in his quiver is priced on its own terms, and the average taken after.

The one exception is a horse. When a rider bears down on him, a man with a spear reaches for the spear — every time, and so does every man beside him who has one. So against cavalry the choice narrows to his polearms, and if he has none he swings what he has and hopes. This is also what lets infantry finally **set a spear against a charge**: a braced polearm lands six-tenths again as hard on a horseman, and harder still for the weight of the horse running onto it, which is the answer foot soldiers have had to cavalry for three thousand years and which auto-resolve has never once let them use.

**And the javelins come off his back first.** Half the infantry in Calradia carry a brace of throwing spears or a few throwing axes, and those are not melee weapons — he hurls them while the lines close and then draws steel. So they are kept off the belt entirely and spent in the skirmish as the lines close, where they belong — with the odd one left over still thrown once the lines have met: a throw is a *missile*, priced on the energy his arm actually puts into it, going to the mass of the man and meeting a shield held up against something in flight.

The count is not a guess — it is the item's own stack size, two javelins or three axes — and it is the whole reason the throwing phase ends. A skirmisher is terrifying for twenty seconds and then he is a man with a knife. That is exactly how it should feel, and it is the first time auto-resolve has let him be either.

**A man defends himself, and how well depends on his training.** A blow is not simply thrown at a target — it is answered. Every melee blow is met by a defense roll, and what a man rolls turns on his own melee skill and on what he has to defend with: an easy, high chance behind an intact shield, roughly twice as hard with only a weapon in his hands, and never quite nil even for a raw levy with neither. A defense that succeeds either **blocks** — a shield eats the whole blow, a weapon merely deflects it — or, when he out-fights the man swinging at him, becomes a **parry**, and he lands a counter-blow of his own. Out-fighting a man turns his defenses into ripostes; that is what training *is*, and it is what finally makes a veteran's skill worth something in auto-resolve beyond the gear he happens to be wearing.

This is why nothing about a shield is a flat number any more. It decides *which* chance a man rolls rather than skimming a share off the blow, and a shield that has been battered to kindling drops him to the bare-weapon chance for the rest of the fight. A steel round shield and a wooden one look identical on paper (both are the same span) but differ five-fold in how much punishment they take, and that is what separates them here.

The exceptions are as pointed as the rule. **A charge cannot be blocked at all** — there is no getting a board in front of a lance at the gallop. An archer ridden down by cavalry keeps barely a quarter of his defense and gets no parry whatever, which is the classic death of unsupported bowmen. A man on a horse defends a little worse than the same man standing, shield and all — he is managing an animal with one hand. And an archer minding his own shot gets the board up late against the arrows coming back at him.

**Arrows are answered by the shield alone** — skill has nothing to do with it, and against a missile the same shield does about a third better than it does against a swordsman. An arrow comes from one known direction and arrives on its own; a man gets the board up and it sticks there. A swordsman feints, comes round the edge, and waits for the shield to drop. This is the whole reason a line advances under fire from behind its shields.

**And a shot can simply miss.** Auto-resolve has never once let one: every arrow it loosed found somebody, and the only thing that could stop one was a shield in the way — so the arm was priced at what a bowman would be worth if he never missed in his life. Now a shot rolls to hit before it is a blow at all, and a shaft that goes wide meets no armor, splinters no shield and kills no horse. What decides it is his training above all — accuracy is the whole of what an archer's training *is*, so a Fian's shafts find men where a levy's find dirt — and then what he shoots (a crossbow is the one ranged weapon in Calradia a conscript can point and loose; a sling is the least accurate thing on the field), how far (a volley arcs into a moving formation and scatters; a closing skirmish is a flat shot at a man he can see), whether he looses from a moving horse, and whether he is shooting at one. A thrown javelin is exempt — that is a committed throw at a man in front of him, not a shaft arcing in from two hundred yards.

**Where a blow lands depends on who threw it.** Two footmen are eye to eye, so it is the chest and shoulders and arms that catch it, the head often enough, and the legs almost never. But a man on foot hacking upward at a horseman finds the rider's *legs and lower body* at his eye level — while the rider, cutting downward, finds the footman's *head and shoulders*.

*Unless he has a spear.* The legs are where a footman's reach ends, not where he wants to strike: a polearm gives him back the height the horse took, so he sets it at the rider's chest and face and does not stoop to the animal's shins. A spearman at a horseman throws the same spread two footmen trade. Only the man *without* a spear is reduced to hacking at legs — which means a horse's barding is worth a great deal against the infantry who have no polearm and much less against the ones who do.

**And a blow at a rider finds the horse or the man, never both.** A footman's stroke takes the animal a little under half the time; a horseman fighting another horseman is aiming at the man he means to unseat and rarely wastes a stroke on the mount; an arrow is loosed at the mass of the rider and only about one in five takes the horse instead. A blow that finds the horse wears the horse *alone* — its own pool, met through its own barding — and never touches the rider, his armor or his defense. A blow that finds the rider meets **his** armor, not the barding, because the barding is the horse's and the horse was not hit.

**And a battle has a shape now — it is not one long brawl.** It has three acts, and auto-resolve has only ever known about the third: the **volley**, while the lines are far apart and the bowmen have the field; the **skirmish**, on the ground between them; and then the lines meet, which is the part everyone already knew about and the least interesting of the three.

*The skirmish is where the javelins fly and the horse fight the horse.* A man does not hurl a spear at somebody a bowshot away — he carries it across the open ground and throws it when he is close enough, and then it is gone and he is a man with a knife. And each side's cavalry ride out at each other well before the foot are anywhere near, which is what cavalry have always done and what auto-resolve has never let them do: it held every horseman back until the infantry lines collided and then threw him into the press, where a horse is worth least.

*The archers get their volley — and in it, nobody else does anything at all.* A bowman is doing the only thing he is for, and the man walking toward him is a bowshot away with his shield up, landing no blow because there is nothing within reach to land it on. This is the whole of what auto-resolve never modelled: it threw archers into a melee at contact range and wondered why they were bad. How long the approach lasts is a question about the ground — six rounds across open country, three through a wood or over a river, **twelve up a siege ladder** with everyone on the wall shooting at you the whole way, and **two in a village**, where there is barely any ground to cross at all. Those are a full-sized battle's figures; a field or village fight of fewer than a couple of hundred men closes faster, down to a single round.

*And the opening rounds belong to the defender* — two in a full-sized field battle, fewer in a small one. He is standing on his ground with the whole field to shoot across; the attacker is still coming, too far out to answer, and eats it. That is what it means to advance on a prepared position. A siege prices the same thing its own way: on the approach the wall shoots back five arrows for every one the attackers loose, and an attacker who cannot reach the wall breaks off.

*And then they run out.* Every archer carries the arrows his own kit gives him, and every shot spends one: the stack's quiver is its living men times their arrows, and a fallen archer's arrows fall with him. When it is empty he is a man with a knife, and his armor was never meant for that. Because the pool grows with the stack faster than the shots it can loose, a big set-piece line shoots for far longer than a roadside handful — and the long battle is still exactly where the arrows run out. The one exception is a man on a wall: he is not shooting from his quiver, he is shooting from the town's stores, and those were filled before the siege began.

*The cavalry charge, and charge again.* Weight and speed are not a thing a rider has for the whole battle or spends in one shock: he comes in at the gallop, kills, is hemmed in, backs out and comes round again. So a **share of his blows** carry the horse behind them and the rest are a man swinging a sword from a saddle — a coin thrown at every stroke, for as long as the fight lasts. How weighted the coin is depends on the ground, the same ground the horse archer needs to ride away on: on the open field a little over half his blows land at the charge, in a wood a little fewer, and in a village street about one in six. A wall or a deck has no charge in it at all, and no horse either. It also depends on there being a crowd to break: a charge wants a press of standing men to ride into, so it is thinned by how many of the enemy are still on their feet, and as his foot are killed the charges come less freely. Horses die, and when one does its rider is a man on foot in cavalry harness.

*And whose turn it is depends on the arm.* Vanilla picks both the man who swings and the man he swings at **uniformly at random from the whole side** — so a footman in the front rank was as likely to "hit" an archer three ranks back as the man in front of him, and an archer was chosen only as often as archers happen to be common. Selection now respects the phase and the arms: the bows act in the volley, the horse and the javelins in the skirmish, and every striker reaches for the enemy he could actually reach — foot for the front line, archers for the massed foot, cavalry for cavalry in the open and for the enemy's shooters once the lines have met. A horseman once engaged lands more blows than a footman, because he rides in, kills, backs out and comes again where the foot grind at one man; a foot archer once overrun lands far fewer, because the enemy is on him and he is drawing a sword or dying. It is a weighted preference and never a hard filter — an arm that is absent is simply passed over, and no blow is ever lost.

**Training counts.** A master with a blade lands what a recruit only swings — up to three times as much, on RBM's own skill curve. Under vanilla's rules, where proficiency mostly buys handling and speed, it counts for much less.

**Every soldier has hit points.** Vanilla gives a line trooper no health at all. His life is one coin-flip per blow: eight damage against a hundred hit points is not eight points off a bar, it is an eight per cent chance the man is simply *gone* and a ninety-two per cent chance the blow never happened. Nothing accumulates — a veteran in plate who has been hacked at for twenty rounds is as fresh as the moment he arrived, and a recruit's lucky swing kills a champion outright. Only heroes ever got a real pool. Now every man keeps one for the length of the battle, so men die in the order they are worn down and twenty grazes finally add up to a corpse instead of twenty separate near-misses. Battles get less swingy, the better army wins more reliably, and every part of the equipment model bites harder: armor that halves a blow now genuinely **doubles a man's life** instead of halving a lottery ticket. A trooper's pool is also widened somewhat beyond his native hundred, because a simulated battle compresses a whole fight into a few thousand blows and each was landing far harder than a real one.

**A beaten army can break and run.** Vanilla's auto-resolve routs a side only when its *standing campaign morale* falls to nearly zero — and that number never moves during a simulated fight, so every auto-resolved battle in the game grinds on to annihilation. With the equipment model on, that morale rout is switched off — it never fired anyway. With routing on, a side that is being **butchered** — worn down to a remnant of fewer than fifty men standing, having lost a larger share of the men it marched in with than the enemy has, and having taken real losses of its own — may break each round, more likely the more lopsided the slaughter becomes. It is casualty share and not headcount deliberately: a small party that merely *starts* outnumbered has taken no losses yet and should not evaporate in round one, and eighty knights beating three hundred recruits are behind on bodies while plainly winning. The break runs through the game's own rout, so the fugitives survive and the pursuit and the rewards all behave. Sieges are left to vanilla. **This is off by default** — it is a real change to how map battles end, so it is yours to switch on.

**A wall is worth standing on.** Vanilla folded the whole of a siege into a flat power bonus, which RBM has to take back out because it is pricing the blow itself now. So the defender's edge is paid in **dice** instead: on the wall he turns aside a third more of what is thrown at him, and in an exchange of shot the man firing *up* at a battlement misses appreciably more while the man firing *down* misses less. That is what a wall actually does — it does not make a man stronger, it makes him harder to hit and gives him the better shot.

**And the men leading the formations are the men who would lead them.** Bannerlord runs two separate perk tracks: a **commander's**, which applies to everyone he brought, and a **captain's**, which applies only to the formation that hero personally leads. Auto-resolve gets the first properly and the second barely at all — its one channel is a *count* of the side commander's captain perks, flattened into a percentage for the whole army, which throws away what each perk actually does, ignores the majority of them (it tests only perks whose primary role is Captain, and most declare it as their secondary), and credits nothing whatever to the companions who would be leading formations in a real battle.

So the sim builds the formations auto-resolve never had, buckets each side's men into them, and appoints a captain over each by porting the game's own assignment rule — your Order of Battle is honoured for your side — then asks each captain for his real perks through the game's own perk helper. Vanilla's flat count is lifted back out, because it is the same thing counted twice. The commander's **hit-point** perks come back too: Thick Hides, Hardy Frontline, Well Built, Hard Knock, Unwavering Defense, Picked Shots and a doctor-lord's Minister of Health, worth upwards of 28 points to a well-led line — every one of which fires in a battle you fight by hand and none in a battle you press the button on.

**And sending your troops now sends you.** A field battle you lead yourself spawns you on the ground and risks you like anyone else, but a battle you *send troops* to has always mustered them with the main character quietly left out: your soldiers fought and fell, and you were simply not there. Every AI lord beside you had no such shield — each one is mustered, swings, and can be wounded or killed. That asymmetry is gone. Send your troops and you go with them, an ordinary hero in the muster: you strike with your own kit, you can be struck, and the surgeon rolls for you exactly as he rolls for every other lord.

**What else has changed, and what has not.** Vanilla's terrain table — the one that made cavalry worth a quarter more in the open and archers worth half as much defending a wood — is lifted out of every blow: a horseman's edge is his horse and his lance, which the kit already prices, and a siege is priced by the model's own siege handling. The leader's Tactics no longer hands one side a flat bonus; it counts at half vanilla's rate, the striker's general against the struck man's. The surgeon's skill still decides whether a downed man is dead or merely wounded. Vanilla's morale no longer multiplies a blow's damage — a man's kit, his training and his hit points decide what a blow does — and, as above, its rout is off while the model runs, so whether a side *breaks* is down to the routing setting.

**Turning the model off turns all of it off.** `SimulationEquipmentEnabled = 0` — or a power weight of zero, which is the same thing — does not just restore tier-priced blows. It stands the whole overhaul down: vanilla's morale multiplies blows again, the flat hundred-point lottery decides every man, arm-blind random selection comes back, the rout never fires, and the player is spared from his own auto-resolve exactly as the base game spares him. Off means off, and what you get is the battle Bannerlord would have fought without RBM installed. (The strength figure the player and the AI are *shown* — party strength, and every AI judgement of whether a fight can be won — is a separate switch, `StrategicPowerEnabled`, and stays on.)

**You can check it rather than trust it.** With logging on, every battle on the map is written to `logs/simulation/` exactly as it was fought — who stood on each side, what they carried, how it ended, and then every blow of it as it landed, with what the man was doing at the time, what armour he met and what the model made of it. Not a replay of the battle: the battle.

It writes out the baselines it measured, every troop's kit item by item, and then **the battle itself, blow by blow** — round by round, every man who swung, what he was doing at the time (shooting, hurling a javelin, charging, setting a spear against a horse, or just walking into arrows with nothing to answer them), what armor he met, what the shield turned aside, what vanilla alone would have hit for, and what the model made of it. That last part is the only place a battle's *story* can be read: the averages tell you it went one way, but only the blows tell you the archers ran dry in round fifteen and were cut down with knives in their hands.

A handful of figures in all this are judgement rather than measurement, and they are gathered in one place — `AUTO_RESOLVE.md` — so they can be argued with rather than hunted for: how often a man gets his shield in the way and how often he turns a defense into a counter, how often a shot goes wide, how much a man achieves while walking into arrows, what a spear set against a horse is worth, how often a horseman finds room to charge, how a blow at a rider divides between the horse and the man, and how the blows distribute across a man's body. Everything else is read from the game's own equations and item data, or measured from its own troop roster.

**`AUTO_RESOLVE.md` documents the whole of it** — including the exact difference between the RBM Combat and vanilla paths, and which terms are deliberately left out of the baseline and why.

## What this changes about how you play

Battles now pay for your army's growth directly. A hard-won victory over well-equipped enemies funds a wave of upgrades that would otherwise have drained your treasury. Grinding looters funds nothing, because looters wear nothing worth taking. Sitting still costs you — an idle army eats and drinks its savings. Losing men loses their share of the purse with them, and a stack wiped out leaves only what its comrades can carry off the field.

The AI plays by the same rules. Lords' parties accumulate spoils, get discounted upgrades from them, and their troop quality now tracks how well their wars have been going.

And the gear you buy your men now decides battles you never watch. Sending troops in is no longer a gamble on their tier — a well-found company beats a ragged one on the map for the same reasons it beats them on the field, so kitting out your men pays even when you never draw your sword. Equally, an army of high-tier troops you have let go to seed will no longer coast on the number beside their name.

## Tuning it

Everything sits in the in-game RBM config under the campaign section, or in the config XML under `/Config/RBMCampaign`:

| Setting | Default | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `TroopUpgradeCostMultiplier` | 1 | Scales the gold and spoils price of every upgrade. **0 turns the whole system off.** |
| `TroopUpgradeSpoilsLootMultiplier` | 1 | How much of a battlefield's salvage your men actually carry off. |
| `TroopUpgradeRequireSupplyTown` | 1 | Troops may upgrade only while a friendly or neutral town is within reach (or while resting in a friendly settlement), and the kit their gold buys comes off that town's shelves. **0 lets them upgrade anywhere, taking nothing off any shelf.** The gold a promotion costs still reaches a town's merchants either way — that money is never destroyed. |
| `TroopUpgradeSupplyRadius` | 30 | How near, in map units, that supplying town must be — roughly a short march. |
| `TroopUpgradeChargeMountValue` | 1 | Price a mounted upgrade's horse and harness into its gold cost instead of consuming a horse item from the baggage. **0 restores vanilla's horse-item requirement.** |
| `TroopLootPiecesPerMan` | 3 | Pieces of kit one soldier can carry away from a field. |
| `TroopLootOverlookChancePerTier` | 0.5 | Chance a soldier walks past a piece of gear, per tier it lies beneath him. |
| `TroopFallenSpoilsCaptureFraction` | 0.75 | Share of a beaten enemy's fallen-and-wounded purse the victors capture; the rest is lost in the rout. |
| `TroopMaintenanceFraction` | 0.005 | Daily field-upkeep cost as a share of a stack's whole kit worth, paid from its purse and overflowing onto your gold. 0 stops maintenance. |
| `IndependentMaintenancePurseFraction` | 1 | How much of that upkeep a **self-funded** clan — one sworn to no kingdom, or a **mercenary** company under contract — meets from its own purses. At the default its men fund their own keep in full; a mercenary's wage into the purse is doubled while the contract holds, so they meet it first and only a genuine shortfall falls to the leader. A sworn vassal's or ruler's men pay none from their purses; their liege bears it all, and that is not configurable. |
| `RecruitMaintenanceDays` | 20 | Days of upkeep a stack freshly recruited from a village or town arrives with already in its purse. 0 seeds nothing. |
| `RecruitDrawsFromSettlementStock` | 1 | A man mustered out of a settlement is outfitted from its market — a town's own, or a village's trade-bound town's — and value-appropriate stock leaves it. A soft draw: it takes what the market has and never blocks a recruitment. 0 arms recruits from nowhere. |
| `TroopLeaderSpoilsCutFraction` | 0.05 | Base share of gathered spoils the party leader skims into his own gold, multiplied by his clan tier + 1, by 1.5 for a mercenary under contract, and by 1 + 0.003 per point of his Roguery. **The one place a purse becomes treasury gold.** 0 leaves the men everything. |
| `TroopSettlementFoodDays` | 20 | Days of rations a stack buys for itself when it reaches a market. |
| `TroopFoodWageFraction` | 0.5 | Share of a day's wage a man will spend on a day's food before calling it extravagant. |
| `TroopSettlementFunWageFraction` | 0.25 | Drink and dice, as a multiple of the daily wage, per day idled in a settlement. At 1 the men spend exactly what the day paid them; above that they eat into their savings. |
| `TroopLuxuryCooldownDays` | 20 | Days a stack waits after an indulgence before it will buy another luxury. |
| `TroopLuxurySpendChance` | 0.02 | Chance per hour idled in a settlement that an over-cap stack buys a luxury off the market. 0 turns luxuries off. |
| `TroopSpoilsHealGoldPerTier` | 10 | Gold a wounded man's stack pays local surgeons, per tier, to mend faster while resting in a settlement. 0 turns paid healing off. |
| `TroopSpoilsHealFractionPerHour` | 0.05 | The most of a stack's wounded that paid healing can mend in a single hour. |
| `TroopSpoilsCapDays` | 20 | Days of keep a stack holds in its purse — this many days of its daily wage and its daily field maintenance together — before it counts itself flush and spends the surplus on drink and luxuries. |
| `ShowInventoryItemWeight` | 1 | Adds a weight column to the inventory and trade rows, carved out of the item name field. With the goods repriced above, weight spans four orders of magnitude and decides what a party can profitably carry. Takes effect on the next game start — Gauntlet caches the parsed prefab. |
| `StewardProvisioningXpPerSpoils` | 0.05 | Steward XP the party leader earns per point of spoils his stacks spend on their own food and luxuries. Config XML only. 0 stops it. |
| `StewardFoodReserveXpPerTier` | 3 | Daily Steward XP per step of food stores (10/20/30/40/50 days' worth), per hundred men. Config XML only. 0 stops it. |
| `StewardMountedFootmanXpPerHorse` | 0.25 | Daily Steward XP per spare mount in the baggage a footman could ride. Config XML only. 0 stops it. |
| `SpoilsLoggingEnabled` | 0 | Writes what the system is doing to `logs/campaign/`, for debugging. |
| `SpoilsVerboseLoggingEnabled` | 0 | Whether that log carries per-stack detail or only party-level summaries. No effect unless logging is on. |
| `EconomyLoggingEnabled` | 0 | Writes the village-to-town goods and food chain — village production, villager dispatches, town rations, and each settlement's daily state — to its own `logs/economy/` folder. Separate from the spoils log: this one is about the countryside, not the troops' purses. |
| `GarrisonRefillLoggingEnabled` | 0 | Writes the garrison-refill AI — depleted lords sent to their own surplus garrisons, the men those garrisons release, AI armies forming and dispersing — to `logs/garrison/`. |
| `SimulationEquipmentEnabled` | 1 | **Detailed auto resolve.** Decides map battles by the men's actual armor, weapons, shields and skill instead of their tier. **0 restores vanilla's auto-resolve entirely** — not just tier-priced blows, but vanilla's morale, its hit-point lottery, its arm-blind targeting and its spared player. |
| `SimulationEquipmentPowerWeight` | 1 | How far kit is allowed to bend a battle. In the default absolute mode it is only an on/off gate; in the older ratio mode (`SimulationAbsoluteDamage = 0`) it is the exponent on the kit ratio, so above 1 widens the gap between a well-found soldier and a ragged one. **0 is the same as turning the setting above off** — the whole overhaul stands down, not just the kit. |
| `SimulationAbsoluteDamage` | 1 | Price a blow at its own real magnitude rather than as a ratio against a typical blow of its arm. Vanilla's surviving factors — its own random spread, the perks — still apply; the tier core is replaced, vanilla's terrain table is dropped, the leader's captain-perk count is dropped while the perk system prices captains itself, and Tactics counts two-sided at half rate. 0 restores the older ratio-against-baseline behaviour. |
| `SimulationAbsoluteScale` | 1 | The one calibration dial of absolute mode: how a blow's real magnitude maps onto the hit points it wears down. Raise it to make blows bite harder and battles kill faster. |
| `SimulationAbsoluteBlowCap` | 1.5 | The ceiling on a single blow, as a multiple of the struck man's hit points — what stops one freak pairing of kit landing a blow many times a man's life. 0 removes the cap. Absolute mode only. |
| `SimulationDefenseSystem` | 1 | **Block, parry and riposte.** A melee blow is met by a skill-based defense roll, and a man who out-fights his attacker counters him. 0 restores the older flat fractional shield-skim. |
| `SimulationShieldBlockChance` | 0.4 | Base share of blows an ordinary shield turns aside, raised by a third against **arrows** (to about half); a better shield turns aside more, by the square root of its quality, up to a cap. 0 makes shields count for nothing against missiles. With the defense system on, melee no longer reads this at all. *A judgement figure — the game does not record how often a man gets his shield in the way.* |
| `SimulationArmTargeting` | 1 | Pick who swings and who is struck by the battle's phase and the arms of service, rather than uniformly at random from the whole side. 0 restores vanilla's arm-blind selection. |
| `SimulationRangedMissEnabled` | 1 | Let a fired shot miss, on the shooter's training, his weapon, the range and whether either man is moving. A missed shaft meets no armor and kills no horse. Thrown weapons are exempt. 0 restores the shot that always arrives. |
| `SimulationRangedMissChance` | 0.35 | What an **untrained** man with a bow misses; every other term works on this. The master dial for the whole arm's accuracy — raise it to put more shafts in the dirt, 0 disables the roll. *A judgement figure, like the shield above.* |
| `SimulationRoundMinutes` | 10 | **How long a map battle takes, in campaign time** — the price of one simulated round in minutes; a siege assault keeps vanilla's own ratio and costs twice it. Not a balance dial: it moves the clock alone, never a blow, a casualty or a phase. Vanilla bills a flat 30 for a round that was a whole chunk of the brawl; an RBM round is a thin phase slice and is priced as one. 0 restores vanilla's flat 30/60. |
| `SimulationSiegeDefenderEnabled` | 1 | **The wall is worth something.** Auto-resolve no longer folds vanilla's siege context into the blow, so the defender's edge is priced here instead — and as better dice rather than a flat power bonus. Off outside a siege. |
| `SimulationSiegeDefenderDefenseBonus` | 1.3 | How much better the besieged man's block and parry rolls are while a besieger is striking him, capped so the wall is an edge and not invulnerability. 1 is no edge. |
| `SimulationSiegeRangedMissSkew` | 1.4 | The height advantage in an exchange of shot, symmetric about 1: the besieger shooting up misses this much more, the defender shooting down this much less. 1 is no skew. |
| `SimulationPerkSystem` | 1 | **Captains and commanders.** The sim synthesises the formations auto-resolve lacks, appoints a captain over each by the game's own assignment rule (honouring your Order of Battle), and asks each for his real perks — replacing vanilla's single flat count of the side commander's captain perks, which is lifted back out so nothing is counted twice. Also restores the commander's hit-point perks to his men, which fire in a battle you fight by hand and in none you press the button on. |
| `SpectateBattlesEnabled` | 0 | Offers to open a battle between two AI lords — a field battle or a siege assault — as a real-time fight you watch and take no part in — no player agent on the field, RTSCamera's free camera the only way to see it. The map battle auto-resolves beside it and reaches its own verdict; what you watch is a copy, written back nowhere. **A measuring instrument, not a campaign feature.** Does nothing without RTSCamera. |
| `SpectateMinTroopsPerSide` | 100 | How big both sides must be before that offer is made at all. |
| `BattleHitLoggingEnabled` | 0 | Writes every blow of a **real** field battle to `logs/battles/` in the same columns the auto-resolve trace uses, so what the simulation claims can be held against a battle that actually happened. Thousands of lines per battle. *(Lives under `/Config/RBMCombat/Global`, not `/Config/RBMCampaign` — it is about the battles you fight yourself.)* |
| `SimulationRoutEnabled` | 0 | **Let a butchered side break and run** instead of being fought to the last man, through the game's own rout, so the fugitives survive. Sieges are left to vanilla. **Off by default** — vanilla's fight-to-annihilation auto-resolve. |
| `SimulationLoggingEnabled` | 0 | Writes every map battle to `logs/simulation/` as it was actually fought: rosters, kit, and the result. |
| `SimulationLogHits` | 0 | And the battle itself, **blow by blow**: every man who swung, what he was doing, what it met and what it did. A large battle runs to several thousand lines. Needs the log above. |
| `StrategicPowerEnabled` | 1 | Prices the strength the player and the AI are *told* a troop is worth — party strength, the encounter screen, every AI judgement of whether a fight can be won — on his actual kit and training rather than his tier. Separate from the auto-resolve switches above, which do not turn it off. |
| `StrategicPowerLogging` | 0 | Writes how each party's strength was priced to `logs/powerCalculation/`. |

The same config section also holds the settings of the module's other systems — buildings and construction, village and workshop production, the market price signal, supply caravans, deserter raiders, AI lords' equipment, the starting-gold multiplier — which belong to those systems' own documents rather than this one.
